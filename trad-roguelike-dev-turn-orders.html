    Trad Roguelike Dev - Turn Orders

    Not a super exciting topic title. But everything revolves around the turn system in a TradRL. There are also many ways to pick a type of turn/action system and each one brings its own advantages and disadvantages.

    Simple turn-by-turn

    This approach is the easiest to understand and implement. It operates like a game of chess. Player A takes a turn and then Player B takes their turn - then repeat. There is no speed of actions or delays of events, each turn is self contained.

    Of course in a TradRL Player B is the PVE elements of the game, enemies, npcs etc. These elements might have their own turn order which indicates if enemy casters go before enemy melee etc. But even when considering this factor those enemies still all process their actions in a single turn.

    Action Duration/Speed Queues

    In this approach every action takes a certain amount of time and all the actions chosen by game entities are placed next to each other and compared to work out the actual order in which they run. For example one enemy might throw a knife (10 speed units to perform) and another might cast a spell (20 speed units to perform). Assuming both start their actions at the same point, the knife will be thrown before the spell is cast.. Although both these actions could still take place in the same enemy turn.

    Units might have individual initiative or speed stats that modify the duration of actions, and in some cases actions started in one turn might not trigger until a following turn.

    This approach is more like a project management milestone graph. It allows for certain interactions that aren't possible in a simpler system. For example a unit that moves 1 tile every 10 time units will catch one that moves at 1 unit every 15 time units. This isn't possible in the simple turn system described above.

    There is a great explanation of this approach by Kyzrati here.

    https://www.gridsagegames.com/blog/2019/04/turn-time-systems/

    Extra Action Systems

    Some games take an approach that sits somewhere between the two already mentioned. TangleDeep for example has an extra-turn stat and related counter on the player. The counter fills up based on the stats and various modifiers, and once full the player gets an extra turn. This approach is similar to other games (Hard West or SMT) where extra turns might be awarded for a kill, a specific attack hitting, or on collecting a pickup etc.

    There's more info on systems like these (and others) here

    https://www.roguebasin.com/index.php?title=Time_Systems

    Even though I appreciate the complexity and flexibility of queue systems I decided to stick to a simple turn approach. Instead of individual movement speed stats I opted to give enemies abilities that can allow them to cross multiple tiles in a single turn, or immobilize a target. This should still allow for certain styles of hit and run combat and limit the amount of kiting permitted.

    Resolving both practically and visually

    When working with any of these systems the actions have to be processed internally and then the results shown to the player. The naive approach is to execute action at their respective points on the resolved timeline and show the results visually as they happen. For a TradRL with immediate movement and interaction results this simple approach might be sufficient. Often all the actions occur so quickly that any delay or visual ordering is negligible. This is particularly applicable to ascii RLs or ones with simple graphics, there are no animations to consider and units tend to move immediately from one tile to the next.

    However, consider a 3D RL (like mine!) where it takes time for any unit in the game to move from one tile to another. The model or sprite might need to trigger its walk animation and transition (or hop) across the screen. Lets say moving 1 tile takes 1 second. If the player moves (1s) and they are faced by 1 enemy who also moves (1s) we would have an even turn cycle (player 1s, enemy 1s). However if we have 10 enemies and they are processed in order we could end up with (player 1s enemy 1sx10=10s). This means the player would have to wait for 10 seconds before returning to their turn. 

    This is actually what happens in turn based games like Xcom, and its acceptable in those scenarios because your own turn involves multiple actions and teils moved etc. But in a TradRL, when 1 turn usually = 1 tile movement, the swop between player and enemy turn needs to be as even as possible. I described the issue here with movement, but the same holds true for actions; a spell might have a lengthy animation or an attack could have a fancy windup. Similarly, if an enemy buffs an ally before they attacks, we should try and respect that sequence visually.

    There is a great article about how you might work with the resolution and representation phases here https://blog.brianna.town/turn-based-gaming-the-atomic-execution-function

    After looking at a number of options the only flexible and 'safe' way I could see to resolve these problems was to execute all turn actions in a single frame, and save the results to a data structure that represents a record of the turn. This means we can resolve all interactions immediately (internally) and then perform them afterwards visually.

    With this in mind it now becomes possible to run all units movements simultaneously. If a 1 tile move takes 1 second, this will always be a 1 second interval no matter how many units we have present. We likely cant run all actions simultaneously though, because a sequence of events is often required (if an enemy A kills enemy B but is then killed themselves we need to to make sure the order of those events is maintained). However this feels more natural as the player will generally be in the middle of a combat encounter and wants to see the order of attacks in a readable way.

    Unlike more immediate graphical representations a 3D world with animations, models and travel time will always mean that larger combat encounters will mean the turn cycle becomes stretched a little, but its a reasonable compromise to make when providing  more detailed and immersive graphical feedback.
