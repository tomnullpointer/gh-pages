    Should you be able to kill the king? - RPG design

    A few days ago I asked the following question on bluesky

    https://bsky.app/profile/tomnullpointer.bsky.social/post/3m5o52cdej22j

    Its a situation I have thought about many times both while playing and designing. Lots of game genre frameworks wont allow this sort of event, and many designers will decide on the answer based on their pre-determined pillars or UX objectives etc. But I am still on the fence about the scenario and was interested in checking out what the range of sentiment was on the matter before I made a decision.

    I was very happy to see that other folk were also interested and I ended up with almost 100 responses (so far) with loads of interesting viewpoints and loads of solutions and examples from games. Rather than let those results vanish along with the thread I thought I might cover some of them here.

    So the basic question is as follows: Should a player be allowed to kill important quest-givers in an RPG, and if so how does the game deal with the consequences. 

    Many RPGs don't attempt to be particularly realistic or "sim" based. In these games its not even a consideration. Most games (almost all the final fantasy titles for instance) have a strict division between combat and exploration, and you cant attack anything outside of those specific delineations. The question I am presenting will never even occur in these games because it has no mechanical avenue to even be a problem.

    However games that lean more towards a simulationist approach (and often more open world sandbox titles) present situations where the player could harm or kill ANY of the NPCs in the world. This can range from deliberately attacking a shopkeeper, to accidentally setting the king on fire with the edge of a misplaced AOE attack. Removing the ability to harm NPCs is valid but might reduce the sense of freedom the game design proposes.

    At this point I am going to assume that we want to at least entertain the idea (rather than outright removing the possibility). So what are the different ways we could tackle the resulting situations.

    I can't include all the replies I had to the original post (you can see them in the link above) But Ill try to summarise some of the main ones.

    Don't let it happen

    This is the default route for most games, but it can be handled in different ways. The most basic approach is simply to make key NPCs invulnerable, or ban any combat actions in the zones where those NPCs exist, in sanctuary zones etc. This solution doesn't even pretend that the action in question is possible, which is fine, but definitely frames the game as a less sim/immersive experience.

    In Oblivion, key NPCs wont die when they are reduced to 0 HP, instead they fall unconscious for a short time and then get back up a few moments later. This does add some sense of drama, since your companions can fall in battle and be unable to support you etc. But there is no permanent consequence, and dead folk standing back up in the middle of a battle can look a little silly.

    Let it happen but Lazarus your way out of the issue

    This is a common solution, by having a mechanism to resurrect dead key NPCs the game allows any quest lines to be continued. The death of the original NPC becomes a bump in chain rather than a real change of path. In many of the Souls games the NPCs can be killed and then 'resurrected' as ghost-like entities, so you can still shop or talk to them. There are a number of variations of this approach that folk outlined in the thread.

    I love this idea! Its got a weird almost valhalla type vibe to it where you could imagine  reincarnation is both a mechanic and part of in-world lore.

    I've only played the first few hours of New Vegas (I should go back) so I wasn't aware of the Yes Man. Its a compromise of scale, everyone is killable but one character, the Yes Man, will respawn. This immortal NPC can then facilitate the rest of quest chains and stop soft locks form happening. It avoids the world being full of ghosts I guess!

    Let it happen and don't say anything

    Some games, particularly older titles would allow the player to brick their game in this way and might not even indicate it had happened. I don't think this is an acceptable approach in contemporary design really. Having broken the quest line in hour one but not knowing until four hours later is quite disrespectful. It also leads to the same sort of issue you can find in 4x games, where you have lost the game but spend 3 hours as a dead man walking. I doubt many player would be happy with this scenario now (or even then!)

    Let it happen but inform the player

    As many posters pointed out, this is what happens in Morrowind.

    I think that this is a better solution (and I love the wording of the warning here!). But its obviously a meta response and totally non-diegetic. There are other games that take this approach, and the solution is usually a pop-up dialogue similar to the familiar "if you open this door you can't go back to the previous zone, are you sure?" It works, and arguably its still true to a simulationist world because there are consequences. But what it usually doesn't do is provide any alternative paths (although morrowind does https://en.uesp.net/wiki/Morrowind:Yagrum_Bagarn_and_Wraithguard )

    Let it happen and respond in world

    This approach is the one that holds most true to the paradigms of real simulation, but is also the one that involves the most work. Effectively all quest chains that can be disrupted by the player killing an NPC must have alternative that can be explored. In theory those alternatives could also get bricked and would need further alternatives, but I don't think either players or designers would be keen on extending that sort of eventuality. 

    If this is the approach taken then there usually needs to be some sort of un-block-able route to end up on. As Cara points out, designers should be aware of how such fallback routes might lead to "less interesting" gameplay and they should either inform the player that they are going to be heading to a less rich solution, OR they should make sure the alternatives are also as interesting.

    For example, if you can kill everyone, then perhaps the game can fill your replacement questing with events that reflect  the guilt/loneliness or outright psychotic consequences of your decisions.

    I've not played Atomfall, but I know that the designers of both this game and Weird West spend a lot of time and effort to allow players to kill everyone and still provide alternative routes that were interesting to pursue. And this attitude leads me to the next point which is perhaps the most crucial.

    What does it feel like?

    One of the reasons Game of Thrones was so memorable is that the the key characters are not immortal. Indeed a significant number of them get killed in a single episode (you know the one). Most games present the world as a sideshow for the players rollercoaster ride, YOU are the focus and everything around you is designed to serve the story that carries you through the game. Its a power fantasy, and nothing disrupts a power fantasy more than part of the set falling over when its supposed to support you.

    This happened to me playing Dark Souls. I was enticed by the uncaring nature of the world... I was the chosen undead, but no one seemed to care. After a few hours I accidentally started a fight with the blacksmith and ended up killing him. I was both shocked and impressed that this was even possible, and when I realised the ramifications meant progress was going to be so much harder I restarted the game. But it didn't upset me, it was another story - a story of failure sure - but a story I rarely found in any other games, and it led to the world feeling even more autonomous and self governing. Obviously this world aesthetic isn't appropriate for most games but It made me feel almost patronised by other games responses to the same sort of situation.

    This response points towards the sort of simulationist promise that my blacksmith murder revealed. Some players won't care that everything is smoke and mirrors designed to ferry them along, but others want the world to live up to the affordances it pretends to have. In doing so it reframes the player experience with more agency, agency to potentially write a bad story, but agency nevertheless. In the responses to my question I was delighted to see a number of folk who embraced this approach "Yeah, let people mess up their games if they want to.

    Of course we should signpost to players that - this will cause a problem - but the freedom to get into those problems is valuable to some players in some types of game-world. The negative consequences of actions can also be an exciting emergent drama.

    As one responder pointed out, it all really depends on the kind of game/world you want to build. But certainly - as an indie developer - this is often more of an ongoing dialogue between what you can build right now and the game that emerges. Asking these questions in public is similar to trying out mechanics in prototypes, its a way to explore and think about design in an evolving context. I often don't know the form a game will take until its already way under development.

    Ultimately I suspect that, as with most game dev, the constraints of time and budget put a stop to designers capabilities to support the consequences of killing the king. If its a choice between a solid guaranteed main path experience or more variable alternatives, we will often chose the former. But in a time where games seem to be taking less risks and following more formulas I can't help but cheer on the ones that dare to let players get off the rollercoaster and make mistakes.
